# State of AI Search (Searchable webinar)

> **RELIABILITY - read before quoting (2026-08-23).** This is a vendor webinar. Roughly a third of the
> runtime is a Searchable product pitch and an "AI Search Accelerator" upsell, all of which is stripped
> from this file. Numbers below are tagged:
> **[DIRECTIONAL]** = vendor's own unaudited study, use as a hypothesis, never as a client-report stat.
> **[SOURCED]** = attributed to a named third party (Profound, Forrester, Gartner, Bain, Meltwater, Adobe).
> The single claim NOT to repeat: **"78% of B2B vendor decisions happen in Claude"** (Donnelly, unsourced,
> and implausible on any read of Claude's market share). Treat it as sales copy.

**One line:** AI search is not one channel. Each engine rewards a different source mix, so a single
blended "visibility score" is misleading, and roughly two thirds of the battleground is content you do
not own.

## 1. Chris Donnelly (Searchable) - engines do not agree

Study basis: 250 brands, 6.1M answers, 5 engines, through August 2026. **[DIRECTIONAL]**

- **14% of questions returned zero overlapping brands** across four engines. One aggregate visibility
  score across models hides more than it shows. Score per engine.
- **67% of pages ranking top-3 on Google never appear in the AI answer.** In 95% of those cases the AI
  read the site and chose a different page or a different source. Ranking is not recommendation.
- **Split of the work: ~1/3 your own site and pages, ~2/3 sources you do not control** (editorial, UGC,
  review sites, Reddit, Trustpilot, social).
- Per answer, an engine **reads ~10 sources and credits ~1**. The other nine shape the answer invisibly.
- **1 in 6 unbranded buying questions has ChatGPT injecting a specific brand into its own search that
  the user never mentioned** ("best running shoes for comfort" -> it searches Asics). Brand memory is
  doing work before any retrieval happens.

### What each engine appears to reward **[DIRECTIONAL]**

| Engine | Leans on |
|---|---|
| ChatGPT | Community and review content |
| Perplexity | The most authoritative sites on the web |
| Google AI Overviews | Existing SERP features and the prior Google index |
| Gemini | The Google stack |
| Claude | Its own training memory, mirrored against **Brave** |

### The Nike case - outranked vs outsourced

For "best trail shoes for rocky and muddy terrain", Nike (~$45-50B/yr) appears **~4% of the time** and
nike.com was **not cited once all summer**. The cited sources are RunRepeat (a lab that buys shoes and
cuts them in half, cited ~70% of the time) and Outdoor Gear Lab. Nike was not outranked, it was
**outsourced** - beaten by more relevant source material. This is category-by-category; do your own.

### Practical moves

- **Restructure pages around the actual questions you track.** Searchable's own site: same information,
  re-structured around questions, **2.6x more citations in 4 days**. **[DIRECTIONAL]**
- **Track fewer models, deeper.** Pick the engines that match your buyer rather than tracking all five
  shallowly. B2B enterprise skews Claude and Copilot; B2C/ecom skews ChatGPT, Gemini, AI Overviews.
- **Prompt volume matters.** 300-500 tracked prompts cannot cover a real catalogue of products or
  services. You need enough to see the source list behind top, middle, and bottom of funnel.
- **The 10/80/10 frame for using AI:** 10% hard focused work up front (context, strategy), 80% delegated
  to the model, 10% you owning the outcome line by line. Failure mode is trusting the model too early.

### On attribution

Adobe found **1-2% of traffic looked like AI referral, but ~20% of people who requested a demo said they
came from AI search.** **[SOURCED - Adobe, as relayed]** Under-attribution is structural: an answer inside
the chat replaces the five tabs you used to open. Demand did not move, the clicks did.

## 2. Andrew Monu (VP Marketing, LinkedIn) - the strongest section

LinkedIn published a companion guide, "Unlocking AI Search Visibility: The B2B Marketer's Guide to
LinkedIn". These numbers come from LinkedIn's own testing on its own platform.

### The market context

- **94% of buyers use generative AI for research** **[SOURCED - Forrester]**
- **Gartner predicts traditional search volume drops ~50% within a couple of years** **[SOURCED]**
- **LinkedIn's own non-branded search traffic declined up to 60%.** Not a hypothetical for them.
- AI search is a top priority for **90% of CEOs** **[SOURCED - Forrester]**; **80% of VPs/CMOs** are asking
  about AI visibility, but only **30% of B2B tech marketers feel prepared**, and **51%** blame lack of
  internal knowledge.

### Buyability and the emotional threshold

Average B2B buying group: **13 stakeholders, 9 external influences**, often doubling when AI is in the
loop. **34% of enterprise B2B buyers rank self-protection above price or features** **[SOURCED - LinkedIn
+ Bain]**. The buyer's number one emotional question is *can I defend this decision if it goes wrong?*
Being "buyable" means clearing an emotional threshold, not just a rational one.

### The credibility stack

Brand sets the point of view -> employees humanize it (**6x engagement** when employees activate as
creators) -> customer advocates validate it (**4x more influential than product claims**) -> creators
scale it (**84% trust brands more when creators demonstrate and review**).

### The LinkedIn citation data - the headline finding

- **LinkedIn is the #1 most cited domain for professional queries in AI search** **[SOURCED - Profound 2026]**
- LinkedIn content scores high on **semantic similarity**: when an LLM cites LinkedIn it tends to quote or
  paraphrase closely, so your framing survives intact.
- **75% of LinkedIn citations come from individual member profiles, not company pages.**
- **CEOs account for only 8%** of cited content; founders similar. **Job title matters less than expertise** -
  individual contributors can earn visibility on par with executives in a niche.
- **Articles generate ~60% of LinkedIn content-type citations; posts ~40%.** They are complementary, not
  competing. **Articles earn authority, posts distribute it.**
- **95% of LinkedIn citations come from original posts, not reshares.**
- Authors with **~2,000+ followers** get surfaced more often (follower count proxies for expertise).
- LinkedIn newsletters are treated by models **as if each edition were a standalone article**. LinkedIn's
  "This Week in Marketing" passed 1M subscribers in year one and is their top weekly performer.

### Format specs

| Format | Length | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Article | 800-1,200 words | Thought leadership, frameworks, deeper analysis. Primary citation source |
| Post | 200-300 words | Timely insight, conversation starter. Secondary pathway to the article |

**Workflow:** publish one strong article as the citation anchor -> break it into 3-5 posts, one idea each
-> use post engagement to test which angles land -> fold the winners into the next article.

### The four pillars of cited LinkedIn content

**1. Structure for retrieval.** Frame around a real question. Lead with keywords in the title/opening.
Use direct-question subheadings, answer each in 1-2 sentences, add a summary or checklist.

Of the top 24 most-cited LinkedIn articles: **100% used bullet lists and numbered items, 75% named
specific companies and tools, 67% included hard numbers and data.**

**2. Establish credibility.** Topical consistency beats scattered posting. Original insights, proprietary
data, first-hand experience.

**3. Drive engagement.** Highly cited content typically earns **10+ thoughtful comments**. Reply to keep
the thread alive.

**4. Freshness and discoverability.** Articles weekly, posts 2-3x/week. Refresh old content with inline
edits. Include dates where relevant ("top AI tools for 2026"). **Check your profile is public and comments
are enabled** - if the model cannot access it, it cannot surface it. **48% of cited content is under three
months old vs 12% for older content** **[SOURCED - Meltwater]**.

### The answer-block format

Start each major section with a **30-80 word answer block** that directly addresses the heading, then
follow with context, examples, sources. Optionally label it "Quick answer". Same logic as the SEO pillar
and waterfall approach: definitive answer first, structured breakdown after.

### Measurement - the part most teams get wrong

- Median time for Claude and ChatGPT to cite a new page: **~7 days. For 90% of pages it is 37 days.**
  **[SOURCED - Profound]**
- Three reasons retrieval lags: indexes refresh on their own schedule; models need multiple corroborating
  sources before registering consensus; models update independently, so **visibility moves in steps, not
  a gradient**.
- **The most common mistake is declaring failure at week three.** Allow 30 days minimum before judging
  against baseline.

**Three-category framework:**

| Category | Examples | Cadence |
|---|---|---|
| Leading indicators | Post impressions, reactions, comments, shares | Weekly. Predictive, not proof |
| Outcome metrics | Citation count, brand mentions, share of voice | Monthly+. These lag |
| Diagnostic patterns | Top-performing content formats, high-performing prompt types | Use to decide what to change |

Also measure **sentiment and downstream business impact**, not citation count alone.

## 3. Joe Hale (Verde) - the ecommerce play

Position: **"It is not the death of SEO, it is a rebirth."** Searches for "SEO agency" globally are at an
all-time high; ~15B searches/day across a far broader surface set (Google, ChatGPT, Copilot, TikTok,
Reddit, YouTube).

### Sentiment as a content engine

Pull negative sentiment themes out of AI answers about the brand and turn each into content **and** into
a business fix. Worked example: a fashion client showing "sizing can be inconsistent" as a recurring
negative -> build better size guides by fit and product type, plus international sizing -> reduces
returns, lifts conversion, and corrects the narrative the model repeats.

**Route the same data outside marketing.** Customer service themes to the CX team. Pricing concerns to
founders (build a "why it costs this" FAQ). Repeated out-of-stock complaints to the buying team. Competitor
citations where you are absent to PR. Cross-reference with internal site-search data to feed product
development. This is how an agency stops being a monthly traffic report.

### Category page depth

**"If you don't talk about your business as X, how can you expect to be discovered as X?"**

A sportswear store with a tops page, a shorts page and a socks page is not targeting "running socks" or
"pilates leggings". Go deeper on collection and category pages.

Paul Smith worked example: built a designer-wedding-suits page targeting the luxury/designer searcher
specifically, not mass-market prom. Result: **+67% YoY clicks from luxury, premium and designer non-brand
searches** across the approach, and the new page alone generated **~100,000 impressions** in ~6-8 months
from a standing start. It now surfaces top-3 in ChatGPT for "designer suits for weddings" and appears in
Gemini alongside Tom Ford and Ralph Lauren. **[DIRECTIONAL - agency-reported]**

Separate ecom client: category-page approach drove **~3.5x YoY non-brand clicks** peak vs peak.

### Did AI kill blog content?

No - it devalued **meaningless** content. SEO spent years producing generic posts that drove clicks and
no revenue. The shift: use AI-visibility data to decide **what format** the content should be, not just
what topic. If the data shows ranked lists appear in 30% of responses for your prompt set, build a ranked
list. If the sources shifted from Reddit toward GQ, that is a digital PR brief, not a blog brief.

## 4. Luke Harries (ElevenLabs) - launches, agent experience, org design

**Treat your website as a product.** It is your highest-performing salesperson and it touches every lead.
Most companies chronically under-invest. ElevenLabs staffs a full website team.

> "Your website is a billboard, your product is a tool." Billboard = design, copy, attention, big visuals.
> Tool = stripped back, opinionated, gets out of the way.

Pattern: interactive hero that delivers the **wow moment immediately**, more SEO-optimised depth below.
Goal set for every page: make each individual landing page good enough to go viral on its own.

**Launches as an AEO tactic.** New AI capabilities create genuinely viral moments (their first speech model
was the first AI voice that could laugh). Launches drive the initial backlinks and branded search that get
new pages indexed and ranking. Three tiers: new product lines and fundraises / major features and case
studies / incremental refreshes.

**Keyword choice is a launch decision.** For Scribe they debated "transcription" (higher volume, but
polluted with DNA/RNA biology intent) vs "speech to text" (lower volume, correct intent). They chose speech
to text, then discovered real-time and non-real-time speech-to-text have non-overlapping result sets, so
they split it into **two launches with two landing pages**.

**Agent experience - "search 3.0".** Search 1.0 = ten blue links. Search 2.0 = agents recommending to
humans. Search 3.0 = the agent searches, selects, and buys autonomously. What they built for it:
API-first so agents can do everything the UI can; **serving markdown versions of pages** (this measurably
increased ranking, and markdown page requests are climbing); an llms.txt; MCP servers. AEO is one of their
fastest-growing channels. Watching x402 and Stripe Agent Commerce for agent-initiated payment.

**Org design.** Each person owns one KPI, cascading up to a north star (self-serve revenue + B2B pipeline).
Grow headcount in proportion to the channel's revenue. **The repeated mistake: leaving SEO/AEO as a
fraction of a person for too long.** Get to one full-time obsessive as fast as you can, then let it become
a team. Their SEO team: SEO data lead (the specialist), SEO content lead, 3 full-stack engineers, website
designer, plus a separate brand website team and a CRO/growth team.

Hiring: **specialists** are sourced by finding companies that are great at the thing, identifying who owned
the inflection point, and screening for enthusiasm and work ethic. **Generalists** are hired via calibrated
"obstacle courses", screening for copywriting and quantitative analysis.

## 5. Dan Slagen (Zapier) - the operating model

- **The shift is behavioural before it is technical.** Committing out loud to "GEO-first content" and
  building the reporting muscle mattered more than any single tactic.
- **From writing to creating.** If LLMs pull from many sources, you need presence beyond your own site.
- **The on-site vs off-site bet is genuinely hard**: build too much off-site and an algorithm change means
  you rented space and the landlord raised the rent.
- **Cut volume, go deep.** Competitors can copy your headline, not your depth. Find where you have the
  right to win.
- **Reset your success metrics.** They had content attribution tuned down to revenue; AEO forced them back
  up the funnel to visibility, traffic, and engagement first. Admitting that was necessary, not a regression.
- **Lossless marketing** (credit: Joe Herrens). Like copying a VHS tape, product truth degrades every time
  it is re-translated across docs, decks, campaigns and channels. Build one source of truth.
- **The monthly narrative meeting.** Product marketing leads a 30-day look-back and sets the next 30-day
  narrative. Few people, high seniority, CEO present. An agent pre-pulls what happened in the market, with
  competitors, with customers, inside and outside the company. Then: does anything change our positioning?
  **Slagen's strongest recommendation.**
- **The "brain".** A shared context layer at company, function, and team level, so people are not each being
  coached by an isolated sycophantic model pointing them in a thousand directions. Layer 1 of AI
  transformation; then tools/automation, governance, harness.
- **The brain is only as good as what it can see.** Zapier tracks the percentage of Slack messages sent in
  public channels vs DMs. Slagen went from 51% to 98% public. Private work is invisible to the brain.
- **The build-a-thon.** Three weeks to learn any coding agent, five coaches on offer, zero tracking of who
  used them. Result: 50+ builds across 9 themes, from dashboards to a full conference-management product
  built by one person in ~2 weeks. Output was a **three-tier map of the team's AI capability** he can now
  assign work against.
- Hiring now includes an AI fluency bar, evidence of having led AI adoption, and skills tests designed to
  **observe how** a candidate uses AI, not whether they do.

## 6. Jonathan Kvarfordt (1mind) - instrument both sides

The core idea: track **what the models say about you** (AI-visibility tool) *and* **what buyers say to the
AI on your own website** (conversational agent logs), then pattern-match the two.

- Analysed ~500,000 anonymised human-to-AI conversations. Classic marketing says 5% of a market is in-market
  at any time; in AI conversations on-site, **~27% showed buying signals**. **[DIRECTIONAL]**
- **94% of B2B buyers use AI search inside their buying motion** **[SOURCED - Forrester]**, so people who do
  reach your site arrive deeper and ask harder questions. Build research-depth content, not just awareness.
- Average AI search query ~5.5 words; their on-site agent averaged 6-10 words with ~23% direct questions.
  The two vocabularies converge, so on-site conversation logs are a legitimate prompt-research source.
- **Conversation logs are a first-party dataset almost nobody is mining.** Beyond pricing and the basics,
  the patterns tell you competitor comparisons, objections, and vocabulary to optimise for.
- Technical audit of their top 100 pages found **0/100 with a canonical tag, 59/100 with no Article schema,
  97/100 with no breadcrumb schema.** Even AI-native companies fail the fundamentals.
- Reported results over ~90 days: citations from ~100 to ~800-900; mentions from ~3,000 to ~14,000; visibility
  11.5% -> 18.4% blended branded + unbranded. **[DIRECTIONAL - vendor case study]**
- Source mix showed LinkedIn rising, YouTube growing hard, Reddit **declining** - which drove a decision to
  produce far more YouTube content.
- **Sentiment as a briefing tool:** spotting a recurring negative narrative in AI answers, then briefing the
  CEO on the five counter-points to hit in upcoming podcast appearances.
